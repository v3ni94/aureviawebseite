In depth · Dental labs

When a single client determines your year

Back to Factoring for Dental Labs

Dental labs have a risk profile that differs fundamentally from that of medical practices: they operate in a B2B business with few, but large, client practices. A lab with a handful of regular client practices often generates a high share of revenue from a single practice. If that practice fails, it is not one invoice that is lost, but a basis of existence. Under non-recourse factoring, this risk transfers to the factor within the agreed limits. Given this debtor structure, that is typically more important than the time advantage.

You pre-finance twice over:

You pay for high-gold alloys, zirconia, titanium, veneering ceramics and implant components at the time of purchase. Production lead time is added on top, then your client practices' payment terms. Several months therefore regularly pass between purchasing materials and receiving payment, for materials whose prices you don't control. Every additional order ties up further capital before it generates any return.

Creditworthiness information you wouldn't otherwise get:

As part of the credit-limit review, the solvency of your client practices is assessed on an ongoing basis. For you, this acts as an early-warning system: if a limit is reduced, you know before payment problems become visible. This kind of information is otherwise difficult for a small or medium-sized lab to obtain systematically. To be clear, this is also the limit of the product: beyond the granted credit limit, there is no credit protection. However, you learn the limit beforehand, not only in insolvency proceedings.

Typical situations in everyday lab operations

  • Digital production. Milling units, 3D printing, scanners and sintering furnaces are capital-intensive and simultaneously change staffing structure and turnaround time.
  • Securing skilled staff. Master dental technicians and qualified technicians are scarce; retaining them costs money before it pays off.
  • Material price fluctuations. Being able to buy when prices are favourable secures margin.
  • New practice relationships. A new major client first increases capital tie-up and default risk, only afterwards the return.
  • Succession. Lab takeovers fail less often because of the purchase price than because of the working-capital needs that follow.
What happens with partial payments or instalment arrangements with practices?

These can be accommodated; the specific arrangement is set out in the framework agreement.

How does an existing blanket assignment to my bank affect this?

It must be reviewed, and adjusted if necessary, before the contract is concluded, otherwise the assignments conflict with each other. This belongs in the initial consultation, not in the contract phase.

Assess your concentration risk.

An anonymised overview of your five largest client practices, with revenue share and payment behaviour, is enough for an initial credit-limit assessment.
